# **Game Design Document and Technical Migration Architecture for the Mingming Framework**

## **Executive Overview and Strategic Objectives**

The project designated as "Mingming" represents a highly ambitious synthesis of two deeply engaging but structurally distinct video game genres: the monster-catching role-playing game and the roguelike deck-building card game. Operating conceptually at the intersection of industry-defining titles such as *Pokémon* and *Slay the Spire*, the application entirely replaces the traditional, menu-driven turn-based battle systems typical of monster tamers with a dynamic, localized resource-driven card game architecture. This systemic pivot fundamentally alters the pacing, tactical depth, and core engagement loops of the gameplay experience, requiring players to not only manage the statistical growth of their captured creatures but also rigorously optimize the probabilistic mathematics of a customized deck of algorithmic commands.  
Currently, the Mingming prototype exists as an application built within the Unity ecosystem, heavily reliant on standard object-oriented C\# paradigms, alongside graphical rendering components written in ShaderLab and HLSL. While Unity serves as a highly effective environment for rapid visual prototyping, its architectural defaults—specifically the tight coupling of game state logic to visual rendering lifecycles through MonoBehaviours—present significant barriers to scaling a deterministic, rules-heavy card game. Therefore, the strategic objective of the forthcoming development phase is a comprehensive architectural migration to a React TypeScript technology stack.  
This document serves as the definitive Game Design Document (GDD) engineered specifically to supply precise contextual parameters, mechanical rulesets, mathematical models, and narrative foundations for the autonomous artificial intelligence agents tasked with executing this codebase conversion. By establishing a rigorous functional blueprint, this report ensures the translation process circumvents the systemic vulnerabilities inherent in the legacy Unity repository and establishes a pure, immutable, and highly performant state machine within the modern React ecosystem.

## **Narrative Architecture and Thematic Justification**

The narrative framework of Mingming is not merely decorative; it provides a profound science-fiction setting that elegantly contextualizes the mechanical loops of battling, programming, and deck-building. The integration of narrative and mechanics is crucial for establishing cognitive resonance with the user, justifying why biological creatures are manipulated via programmatic "cards."  
The lore is rooted in a historical timeline devastated by catastrophic climate change and ecological collapse. In response to this existential threat, the visionary founder of the MING initiative, Hope Dunphy, spearheaded the creation of artificial intelligence robots—the titular "MingMings"—initially engineered exclusively for planetary terraforming, atmospheric scrubbing, and environmental restoration. Concurrently, Dunphy discovered Faster-Than-Light (FTL) travel but chose to abandon the technology to focus on saving Earth directly, a decision that created a critical divergence in human history.  
Other scientific factions usurped this abandoned FTL research, opting to construct massive cryogenic colony ships destined for twelve distinct, potentially habitable exoplanets. As ten percent of the global population evacuated for a two-hundred-year voyage, Earth descended into a catastrophic civil war driven by resource scarcity and political abandonment. During this conflict, a critical technological shift occurred: Neil Everret weaponized the previously benevolent terraforming MING robots. By exploiting their self-teaching neural networks, these robots were transformed into highly effective, autonomous combat units, decisively turning the tide of the war. Eventually, Neil and his son Patrick recovered the FTL technology, ending the conflict and transporting the remaining populace to the twelve exoplanets, remarkably arriving ahead of the original, slower colony ships.  
Upon arrival, the hostile conditions of these new worlds necessitated the deployment of the MING units in their original, unweaponized terraforming capacity. The confluence of advanced robotics, newly acquired planetary resources, and established infrastructure propelled humanity into an unprecedented golden age. As generations passed and existential threats dwindled, the societal function of the robots shifted from survival utility to competitive entertainment.  
In the contemporary game timeline, developers and programmers command these AI robots in highly structured tournaments, utilizing "Method," "Function," and "Program" cards to execute offensive and defensive algorithms. This narrative framework ingeniously justifies the card-based mechanics; the player is literally acting as a **Developer**, compiling a deck of executables to govern their artificial combatants in real-time. The ultimate objective for the player is to navigate the planetary landscape, continually optimize their robot's software architecture (the deck), pass twelve rigorous preliminary tests, and ultimately depose the reigning planetary champion. Furthermore, the synthesis mechanics are narratively framed as "learning the architecture" of a defeated MingMing, allowing the Developer to compile a replica using acquired "scraps".

## **Core Gameplay Loops and Overworld Traversal System**

The macroscopic gameplay loop of Mingming is segmented into distinct phases: environmental exploration, deterministic and probabilistic combat, deck management, and algorithmic progression. Translating these phases into a React TypeScript architecture requires a clear understanding of the user interaction paradigms and the underlying state requirements for each segment.  
The environmental exploration phase occurs within a two-dimensional top-down environment utilizing a strict four-directional movement grid. The world map is constructed from defined tile sets, primarily categorized into traversable ground tiles, impassable boundary walls, and hazard zones designated as "Tall Grass". Within a React context, this overworld must be managed as a strictly typed coordinate system (e.g., a 2D array matrix), where the player's position is an X,Y coordinate pair updated via keyboard event listeners dispatching movement actions to the global state store.  
Movement through the "Tall Grass" hazard zones introduces a probabilistic algorithm determining random combat encounters, simulating the unpredictability of wild or rogue AI units operating autonomously in the environment. This requires the React application to maintain an encounter rate variable and execute a seeded pseudorandom number generator (PRNG) evaluation upon every successful movement into a hazard tile. Additionally, deterministic encounters are triggered through interaction systems with stationary non-player characters, referred to as **Developers**. These Developers operate with dialogue boxes and initiation prompts, serving as definitive skill checks for the player's current deck composition and unit levels.  
Outside of active combat, the player is required to interact with specific environmental nodes, such as a "Bench" or "**Terminal**," which serve as dedicated gateways to the deck-builder interfaces. The state architecture must track the player's global coordinates and detect adjacency to these nodes to enable interaction prompts. The separation of the overworld state from the combat state is critical; when an encounter is triggered, the application must freeze the overworld matrix, push the current exploration state to a historical stack, and mount the entirely distinct Combat Scene component hierarchy.

## **The Deck-Building Ecosystem and Party Management**

The deck-building interface is the primary mechanism for strategic customization and algorithmic progression. When accessing a terminal or bench, the player is presented with a comprehensive UI requiring sophisticated state management. The interface demands a scrollable pagination system displaying the player's comprehensive global card collection (the inventory), alongside a dedicated sidebar representing the active deck configuration.  
The React components governing this interface must enforce strict validation logic based on the game's design parameters. Cards can be dynamically added or removed from the active deck, but the system must enforce cardinality limits, actively disabling the addition of a specific card if the maximum owned quantity has been reached in the active configuration. Tooltips are a mandatory UI/UX requirement, providing deep metadata upon cursor hover, revealing elemental types, energy costs, raw power metrics, and specific status applications.  
Party management is equally rigorous. The player's active roster—referred to as the MingMing party—is strictly capped at a maximum of three concurrent units. A critical design constraint dictating the pacing of the game is the prohibition of intra-party switching during active overworld traversal; roster modifications can only be executed at dedicated computer terminals. Furthermore, consumables and restorative items are restricted entirely to the overworld exploration phase. This design choice forces the player to rely entirely on the mathematical viability of their constructed deck and the intrinsic capabilities of their active MingMings during engagements, preventing the circumvention of challenging battles through item spamming.  
The acquisition of new cards and units drives the economic loop. Winning a battle immediately yields new programmatic cards as rewards, expanding the player's strategic toolkit. The acquisition of new MingMing units operates on a bifurcated system encompassing both direct computational synthesis and a sophisticated crafting economy. 
Alternatively, players can engage with the "Scraps" economy. Redundant or mathematically inferior cards can be systematically broken down to generate common Scraps. Furthermore, defeating specific MingMings yields localized, unit-specific data fragments referred to within the narrative as "**Blueprints**" or "Architectures". By aggregating a defined quotient of specific architecture scraps combined with common scraps, the player's character can compile and synthesize a brand-new MingMing unit for their roster at a **Bench**. This elegantly ties the card-acquisition loop directly into the monster-acquisition loop, ensuring that every discrete action feeds into a unified progression state.

## **Combat Engine Subsystems and Mathematical Resolution**

The combat system is the undisputed nucleus of the Mingming experience. It aggressively discards the traditional "select attack from a static menu" paradigm in favor of a highly volatile, localized resource management system driven by the stochastic elements of a drawn hand of cards. Battles scale dynamically in complexity, supporting configurations from isolated one-on-one duels up to comprehensive three-on-three engagements. To resolve visual crowding in multi-unit battles, the UI adopts a "**Step Forward**" paradigm: when a Program is selected, the source MingMing physically lunges forward while valid targets are highlighted, ensuring clear tactical focus.

### **Deterministic Turn Structure and Action Economy**

Combat operates on a strictly deterministic turn-based state machine, meticulously segmented into three distinct operational phases: the Pre-Turn Phase, the Attack Turn Phase, and the Post-Turn Phase. It is imperative that the React application models these phases as discrete action dispatches within its global store, preventing asynchronous race conditions.

1. **Pre-Turn Phase:** The engine initializes the cycle by resetting the energy usage pools for all active MingMings on the current player's side. Subsequently, the system evaluates all active status effects, executing any necessary logical checks and decrementing their duration counters. Finally, the draw mechanic is invoked, continuously pulling objects from the randomized deck array into the hand array until the strict hand limit of nine cards is achieved. If the deck array is depleted during this phase, the discard array must be mathematically shuffled and reassigned as the new deck array before the draw continues.  
2. **Attack Turn Phase:** During this phase, the application grants agency to the player to expend unit energy to play cards. The interaction sequence is highly specific and requires robust UI state tracking: the player must select a valid card from the hand (validating energy costs against the unit's current pool), designate the source MingMing (the unit executing the compiled program), and subsequently target the appropriate enemy or allied unit(s) based on the card's target parameter (Single, Self, Side, or Single,Self). 
To mitigate "Dead Hand" scenarios where a unit lacks the energy to play drawn cards, a player may execute a **Transfer** action: **sacrificing 2 Energy from one MingMing to grant 1 Energy to another allied MingMing.** 
The UI must support right-click cancellation of target designation to prevent accidental execution. Upon execution, the mathematical resolution occurs, and the card is immediately transferred to a discard array.  
3. **Post-Turn Phase:** The phase shift is triggered upon the player explicitly clicking an "End Turn" interface element. The engine then evaluates and resolves any inter-turn mechanics, primarily damage-over-time (DoT) algorithms triggered by specific status effects like Poison or Burn. Crucially, the player's remaining hand is entirely purged and moved to the discard array, enforcing a fresh tactical evaluation for the subsequent turn. Control state is then passed to the opponent.

The opponent is governed by an artificial intelligence module, originally integrated into the repository five years ago. In the React rewrite, this AI must operate as an asynchronous thunk or saga that evaluates the current board state, selects optimal or randomized card plays, dispatches the corresponding actions, and then autonomously triggers the end-turn phase to return control to the player.

## **Status Effect Ecology and State Mutations**

Status effects introduce persistent, turn-over-turn state mutations that fundamentally alter the flow of combat and necessitate constant tactical reassessment. These effects are applied via specific card invocations (classified as 'Status' effect types) and decrement naturally during the Pre-Turn phase or under highly specific programmatic triggers.  
The state management architecture must be highly robust to handle these interactions. The system must natively handle opposing status effects through direct mathematical cancellation. For instance, the application of the "Sharp" status to a unit currently suffering from the "Dazed" affliction will not result in a unit possessing both status objects in its state array; instead, the logic must intercept the application, mathematically nullify both conditions, and remove them entirely from the state.  
The React store must define the following status conditions, each requiring specific reducer logic to handle their unique mechanical impacts:

| Status Condition | Mechanical Definition and Reducer Implementation Directives |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Asleep** | A paralyzing condition that overrides the standard Pre-Turn phase logic. It forcibly sets the afflicted unit's available energy pool to 0, completely disabling card play. This state persists for a maximum duration of 3 turns. Crucially, the engine must attach an event listener to the unit's health pool; the Asleep state is immediately shattered and removed if the unit receives any external damage \> 0\. |
| **Poisoned** | A degrading affliction requiring Post-Turn evaluation. It causes continuous damage evaluated at the end of the turn cycle. Furthermore, it intrinsically applies a base 5% damage reduction to the victim's total offensive output calculation. ### **Water Class Algorithmic Analysis**

The Water archetype is systematically bifurcated into two distinct strategic pathways: "Slow Drip" (a Control archetype) and "Tsunami" (a Combo Midrange archetype). The "Slow Drip" control framework relies heavily on sustained mathematical advantage, prioritizing healing, debuff cleansing, and card draw over raw damage output. Cards such as "Cleanse," "Bathe," and "Whirlpool" allow the player's engine to maintain high health pools while constantly replenishing their hand array, ensuring they always have the optimal programmatic response to the enemy's actions.  
Conversely, the "Tsunami" archetype leverages area-of-effect (AoE) damage and complex energy manipulation. Cards like "Wave" and the exceptionally high-cost "Cannon Ball" (which demands 4 energy resources for a massive 100 power strike across a whole enemy side) heavily incentivize players to build defensive stalls using "Slow Drip" cards until they can unleash massive, board-clearing execution spikes. Furthermore, the Water class possesses highly specific state synergies with the "Asleep" status effect. The combination of "Yawn," "Hypnosis," and "Reguvinate" creates a sophisticated logical engine where a player can disable enemy action economy while simultaneously utilizing the sleep state flag to mathematically double their own healing output, creating an insurmountable health disparity.

### **Fire Class Algorithmic Analysis**

The Fire archetype is fundamentally structured around high-risk, high-reward gameplay loops, split into the "Burn" (Aggro) and "Glass Cannon" (Midrange) archetypes. The Aggro pathway relies heavily on manipulating the staged Burn status effect previously detailed. Cards like "Toats" and "Roast" are explicitly designed to bypass raw damage calculations and instead rapidly escalate the target's Burn state variable to the 3rd Degree, causing compounding percentile damage that bypasses standard defensive statistics entirely.  
The "Glass Cannon" archetype utilizes aggressive self-buffing to exponentially increase burst damage, prioritizing the rapid conclusion of combat. Cards such as "Preheat" and "Rage" apply the "Strengthened" status variable directly to the user, while "Erupt" contains specific conditional logic that capitalizes on this state, dealing its 90-power area-of-effect damage twice sequentially if the user's state object contains the Strengthened flag. The archetype intentionally lacks the sustain algorithms found in Water, offering only highly conditional healing through "Fired Up," which requires the unit to actively be suffering from a detrimental Burn status to consume it for health recovery, turning a negative state mutation into a positive, albeit risky, resource.

## **Legacy Architecture Analysis and Component Critique**

An exhaustive examination of the provided source repository indicates that Mingming was initially constructed entirely within the Unity ecosystem. The repository statistics confirm a codebase composition dominated by C\# (85.2%), interspersed with highly specialized graphical rendering components authored in ShaderLab (12.6%) and HLSL (2.2%). Over the course of its history, encompassing 230 discrete commits across a five-year timeline, the developer introduced various modular systems, notably an Enemy AI logic controller, a persistent player inventory mechanism, and a bespoke Card Editor constructed utilizing Unity's UIElementsSchema.  
While Unity is arguably the premier engine for rapid spatial and visual prototyping, developing complex, mathematics-heavy card games within its standard architectural patterns often precipitates severe, systemic bottlenecks. The most pervasive architectural flaw inherent in typical Unity projects—and highly likely present within this repository—is the reliance on MonoBehaviour lifecycle hooks. Unity's architecture naturally encourages developers to entangle visual rendering logic (e.g., executing a particle system for "Flamethrower," moving a card sprite across the screen) directly with the core mathematical state logic (e.g., deducting health points, shuffling arrays, incrementing status counters).  
This tight conceptual coupling renders automated unit testing of the core game rules nearly impossible, as verifying a simple damage calculation requires booting the entirety of the physics and rendering engine context. Furthermore, C\#'s standard implementation within Unity relies heavily on mutable state objects and arrays. When list contents are modified in place (e.g., DiscardPile.Add(card)), implementing advanced features critical to modern card games—such as deterministic replay saving, complex action undo capabilities, or robust desynchronization protection for potential multiplayer implementations—becomes exceptionally difficult, if not technically impossible. The visual presentation becomes intrinsically tied to the exact millisecond the logic executes, leading to race conditions where UI elements update before animations conclude, or worse, state desynchronizes entirely from the visual representation.

## **React TypeScript Migration Strategy and Architectural Reformation**

Transitioning the Mingming application from a monolithic, object-oriented Unity architecture to a modular, functional React TypeScript stack fundamentally transforms the software from a physics-simulated environment to a high-performance, event-driven state machine. To ensure the success of this rewrite, the autonomous AI agents executing the code generation must adhere strictly to the following architectural paradigms to rectify the legacy flaws.

### **Enforcing Strict Immutability via Centralized State Management**

The paramount architectural directive for the React migration is the absolute, uncompromising enforcement of immutable state. The combat engine, inventory system, and overworld coordinates must be modeled holistically as pure reducer functions. Utilizing robust state management libraries such as Redux Toolkit or Zustand is a non-negotiable requirement for instantiating a centralized, predictable data store.  
When a player interacts with the interface—for example, attempting to execute the "Hydro Pump" card—the React component capturing the click event must not directly manipulate the target's health total or the player's energy pool. Instead, the component must construct and dispatch a strictly typed action payload to the central store. For instance, the payload would specify the action type (PLAY\_CARD), the unique identifier of the executed card (hydro-pump), the specific source entity ID, and the designated target entity ID.  
The centralized reducer intercepts this dispatched action and initiates the logic phase. Crucially, it must first create a deep, structural clone of the current game state tree. Operating entirely on this clone, the reducer validates that the source unit possesses the requisite energy to execute the command. It then applies the complex mathematical damage calculation algorithm previously detailed , deducts the calculated integer from the cloned target's HP, moves the card object from the cloned hand array to the cloned discard array, and ultimately returns this entirely new state object back to the application. React's internal reconciliation engine and virtual DOM will then automatically compare the old state tree against the new state tree, calculating and executing the precise, minimal visual updates required on the client's screen. This architecture guarantees absolute predictability and eliminates race conditions; identical state inputs combined with identical action payloads will perpetually and flawlessly produce mathematically identical state outputs.

### **Decoupling Logic from Presentation: The Headless Engine Paradigm**

The repository rewrite must aggressively adopt a "Headless Game Engine" paradigm. All core logic pertaining to deck shuffling algorithms, probabilistic calculations, combat resolution math, status decrementation loops, and artificial intelligence decision trees must be completely, structurally decoupled from the React component hierarchy. The game engine should exist as a suite of pure TypeScript utility functions or standalone classes capable of executing successfully within a raw Node.js environment, entirely devoid of HTML, CSS, or Document Object Model (DOM) references.  
Within this architecture, the React components exist strictly and exclusively as a presentation layer. A primary BattleScene component would subscribe to the headless engine's state tree, rendering individual \<Card\> interface components based on the contents of the engine's hand array, and \<MingMing\> sprite components based on the field array. By forcefully isolating the mathematical engine from the presentation layer, the development team empowers the implementation of exhaustive, automated Jest unit tests. These tests can rapidly iterate through thousands of simulated turns, covering every conceivable edge case of the complex status effect stacking logic—such as verifying that a script applying the "Sharp" status correctly identifies and cancels out a pre-existing "Dazed" status—without expending computational overhead rendering a single pixel.

The transition from C\# to TypeScript provides a vital opportunity to leverage strict structural typing to catch logic errors during compilation rather than runtime. The raw data matrix provided in the source documentation must be codified into exhaustive, strictly typed interfaces. The AI agents must generate type definitions that cover every aspect of the game's schema.  
A baseline implementation must explicitly define the allowed enumerations: type ElementType \= 'Fire' | 'Water' | 'Earth' | 'Air' | 'Nature' | 'Ice' | 'Light' | 'Dark' | 'None'; type TargetType \= 'Single' | 'Self' | 'Side' | 'Single,Self'; type EffectCategory \= 'Attack' | 'Status' | 'Spell' | 'Heal';  
Subsequently, the foundational card object must be rigorously defined to prevent malformed data ingestion: interface ICardData { id: string; name: string; description: string; element: ElementType; target: TargetType; effectType: EffectCategory; modifier: number; cost: number; statusApplied: StatusCondition | null; rarity: number; }  
By explicitly defining these structural interfaces throughout the Redux store and component props, the TypeScript compiler acts as an automated architectural safeguard. It will immediately flag developmental errors at build time, such as attempting to assign a floating-point damage multiplier to a card explicitly designated purely as a 'Spell' effect type, or attempting to programmatically target an entire 'Side' with a command explicitly hardcoded for 'Self' targeting.

### **Addressing the Non-Deterministic Probability Flaw**

A critical flaw inherent in standard game development patterns is the reliance on non-deterministic random number generation. Mingming includes numerous core mechanics inherently reliant on probability, specifically the random draw of objects to populate the hand up to the 9-card limit , the probabilistic algorithms determining encounters in Tall Grass , and the random AI decision tree logic. Utilizing the standard Math.random() function in JavaScript, or its equivalent UnityEngine.Random in legacy C\#, generates entirely non-deterministic values based on system clock entropy. If the application intends to support deterministic replay mechanics, perfect auto-saving of exact mid-battle states, or network synchronization for multiplayer functionality, relying on non-deterministic RNG represents a fatal architectural vulnerability.  
The React rewrite must explicitly discard standard random functions and implement a strictly seeded pseudo-random number generator (PRNG) library. The core global game state must store the current numerical seed integer. Every single time the headless engine requires a probabilistic operation—such as mathematically shuffling the discard pile array back into the deck array—it must process the algorithm exclusively through the seeded PRNG, which subsequently updates and mutates the state's global seed value for the next operation. This rigorous approach ensures that a highly complex, 40-turn battle can be perfectly reproduced by a different client simply by supplying the identical initial numerical seed.

### **Refactoring Hardcoded Asset Logic via the Command Pattern**

Finally, the source CSV data utilizes boolean and integer flags to dictate behavior. A common anti-pattern in early game development is relying on massive, monolithic conditional switch statements (if card.name \=== 'Squirt' then execute LogicA) to manage specific card mechanics. This reliance on hardcoded names or IDs to execute logic is the hallmark of a brittle, unscalable architecture. If a system designer wishes to add 100 new cards to the compendium, the monolithic switch statement becomes unreadable, unmaintainable, and highly susceptible to regression bugs.  
The React and TypeScript architecture should natively adopt the Command Pattern to circumvent this flaw. Each card in the JSON database should not merely be a static data object, but should contain metadata that references pure, modular algorithmic functions. The central reducer engine maps the string definitions of EffectType (e.g., 'Attack', 'Heal', 'Status') to dedicated, mathematically pure processing functions. These functions ingest the Modifier, Element, and StatusApplied variables directly from the card payload to compute the state outcome dynamically. This data-driven architectural approach ensures that new content, archetypes, and elements can be integrated into the game merely by updating a static JSON file, requiring absolutely zero modifications or recompilations of the core engine codebase, ensuring total scalability for the future of the Mingming application.
